Holoscan channels are built on HoloMQ, a typed shared-memory transport. This
page explains how to read their hot-path measurements for the two payload
families you will use most: small POD values carried inline through the
transport, and Tensors carried as descriptors while the payload bytes stay in
place. Read
[the performance model](/holoscan/sdk-user-guide/performance/performance-model)
first if you have not — the vocabulary here (serialized vs. saturated,
delivery cycle vs. one-way E2E) comes from that page.

The controlled result set below comes from one Release build and one tuned
x86_64 workstation. Later sections break the same run's saturated rows into
per-call costs to show the cost *structure* of each transport path. All values
are measurements, not guarantees for your hardware.

<Note title="You will learn">
- What one controlled x86_64 run measured for HoloMQ and Channel latency and
  throughput.
- How the two Tensor transports differ: same-process sidecar vs. interprocess
  `BufferHandle` descriptor import.
- Why serialized and saturated results must never be compared directly.
- How publish cost grows from 1:1 to 1:2 fanout for both Tensor paths.
- Which provenance must accompany every published result.
</Note>

<Warning>
  **One graph, one vocabulary.** Inside a graph, operators communicate through
  typed ports (`Input<T>`/`Output<T>`) wired with `add_flow()` — the runtime
  materializes the channels for you. The `ChannelRuntime` client API exists
  only for standalone processes outside any graph. Never mix the two: using
  `ChannelRuntime` inside an operator bypasses the compiled plan and its
  guarantees.
</Warning>

## Controlled transport suite

<Note title="How to read the controlled results">
  Each value is the median of three repetition-level results. Channel and
  interprocess HoloMQ latency use 5,000 post-warmup samples, 500 warmup
  samples, queue depth 1, and 200 µs spin pacing. They measure from
  immediately before publication through successful receive completion.
  Throughput is unpaced and saturated; matched POD rows use queue depth 1
  and bulk rows use queue depth 64. The intraprocess HoloMQ control retains
  its benchmark-native batch and mean-iteration semantics. The balanced
  preset does not report p99.9 because its 5,000 latency samples are below
  the suite's 10,000-sample p99.9 threshold.
</Note>

### CUDA stream-ordering control

The device-buffer control separates raw descriptor transport from
stream-ordered ownership. All four rows reuse backing initialized before
timing; they differ only in the CUDA readiness and release protocol applied
to each publication.

| Producer readiness | Consumer deferred release | Logical messages/s | Paced p50 | Paced p99 |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| No | No | 619,057 | 1.358 µs | 1.744 µs |
| Yes | No | 49,557 | Not measured | Not measured |
| No | Yes | 138,870 | Not measured | Not measured |
| Yes | Yes | 16,929 | 5.088 µs | 14.025 µs |

The fully stream-ordered row is the relevant lower-level control when a
producer changes device contents on one stream and a consumer uses them on
another. It enqueues a generation write, a generation wait, and deferred
producer and consumer refcount releases. Its 66.989 µs
pool-acquisition p50 shows that stream-ordered slot recycling, not the
BufferHandle descriptor itself, limits this implementation under saturation.
By contrast, the descriptor-only row needs no fence because initialization
completed before publication.

<Warning>
  The controlled Channel Tensor and ImageT throughput rows above are
  descriptor-only transport measurements. Do not use them to predict a
  workload that writes a fresh device payload every sample. Such a workload
  must retain stream ordering and also includes its producer work. The
  current intraprocess sidecar benchmark does not yet provide a matched
  per-sample CUDA-work row.
</Warning>

## How to read the per-call tables

Two execution models appear on this page, and they measure different things:

- **Serialized**: one thread calls `emit_sample()` and then immediately calls
  `receive_sample()` on the paired endpoint, with exactly one sample in
  flight. The **delivery cycle** is the emit-plus-receive round through the
  transport. This isolates API cost cleanly.
- **Saturated**: publisher and subscriber run concurrently on separate
  threads or processes with no pacing and a queue depth of 64. The **one-way
  E2E p50** runs from the publisher's timestamp to completed receive and
  includes queue residence and scheduling. Because several samples are in
  flight, completed samples per second can *exceed* `1 / E2E` — throughput is
  a pipelined completion cadence, not the reciprocal of latency.

Never compare serialized and saturated results directly; compare cost
*structure* across them, not absolute values.

Every `emit`/`receive` in these benchmarks carries a full `Sample<T>` — the
value plus its `SampleMetadata` — even when the convenience `emit(T)` /
`receive()` overloads are used.

The tables below break the controlled run's saturated rows into per-call
costs. They use queue depth 64, no pacing, and 500 warmup samples, followed by
50,000 measured POD samples or 5,000 measured device Tensor samples. Each value
is the median of three repetition-level results, and every row has the
provenance listed under [Controlled-run provenance](#controlled-run-provenance).

## POD inline transport

POD payloads travel inline: the bytes of `T` and the `SampleMetadata` are
copied into the transport sample itself. The same code path serves
same-process and interprocess deployments — only the queue's process
placement changes.

| Deployment | `emit_sample()` p50 | Successful `receive_sample()` p50 | One-way E2E p50 | Completed samples/s |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Same process, publisher and subscriber threads | 887 ns | 675 ns | 1,752 ns | 1,001,567 |
| Separate publisher and subscriber processes | 886 ns | 657 ns | 1,736 ns | 1,012,752 |

The near-identical intraprocess and interprocess rows are expected, not a
fluke: both use the same typed inline transport path, so crossing a process
boundary costs essentially nothing extra for POD payloads on this host. These
queue-depth-64 rows complete slightly more samples per second than the
queue-depth-1 POD rows in the throughput tables above.

<Warning>
  Do not add the emit and receive medians to reconstruct latency. The two
  calls execute on different threads or processes and overlap with transport
  and scheduling; their sum is not the end-to-end path.
</Warning>

## Tensor transport: sidecar vs. BufferHandle

Tensor payloads never copy their bytes through the queue. Two mechanisms
carry them:

- **Same-process sidecar**: the publisher stores the Tensor handle in a
  runtime-owned sidecar store and publishes a small descriptor. The
  subscriber resolves the descriptor and receives *the same underlying Tensor
  allocation* — no import, no new view.
- **Interprocess `BufferHandle`**: the publisher exports a descriptor
  encoding a `BufferHandle` plus shape, strides, and dtype. The subscriber
  imports the referenced pool slot (cached after first import) and
  materializes a new read-only Tensor view whose lifetime is tied to the
  lease.

### Saturated comparison (1 MiB device Tensor)

| Payload and deployment | `emit_sample()` p50 | Successful `receive_sample()` p50 | E2E p50 | Completed/s |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tensor, same process, sidecar | 1,620 ns | 1,108 ns | 3,044 ns | 537,824 |
| Tensor, separate processes, device `BufferHandle` | 1,650 ns | 1,520 ns | 3,750 ns | 549,331 |

Structurally, sidecar receive reuses the existing Tensor handle, whereas
`BufferHandle` receive imports a pool slot and materializes a new Tensor
view — that is why the interprocess receive p50 is about 400 ns higher even
though its emit is the same. Both rows complete about the same number of
samples per second, because publisher and subscriber pipeline across the
thread or process boundary.

Putting the saturated 1:1 rows from both payload families side by side makes
the cost structure visible at a glance: both POD deployments land at ~1.7 µs,
and the two Tensor descriptor paths sit roughly 1.3–2.0 µs above them.

## Interpretation constraints

Read these before quoting any number:

- **Device rows measure descriptor transport only.** They transport
  descriptors, import pool slots, and materialize Tensor views; they do not
  copy 1 MiB of payload data or execute any real GPU work. CUDA
  generation-ordering waits and stream-ordered deallocation can dominate a
  real stream-ordered interprocess pipeline and are deliberately excluded
  here.
- **Percentiles are post-warmup and successful-calls-only.** p50 values are
  nearest-rank percentiles over individual successful samples after warmup;
  subscriber `kNotReady` polls are not included in the successful-receive
  durations.
- **Saturated E2E is not idle-system latency.** The saturated rows are
  throughput tests; their E2E percentiles include queue residence and
  scheduling. Use the paced latency tables above for one-way latency.

## Recap

- Intraprocess Channel measured 0.964 million `BenchmarkPod64` messages/s
  with 1.701 µs p50 latency; the closest concurrent HoloMQ polling control
  measured 1.124 million messages/s and a 1.409 µs mean iteration.
- In the controlled interprocess 1:1 run, HoloMQ delivered 64-byte values at
  1.095 million messages/s with 0.804 µs p50 latency; Channel delivered
  `BenchmarkPod64` at 0.982 million messages/s with 1.656 µs p50 latency.
- A 1 MiB device `Tensor` reached 549,331 messages/s and 3.133 µs p50 through
  Channel; device `ImageT` reached 407,820 messages/s and 4.231 µs p50.
- Tensor bytes never cross the queue: same-process sidecar reuses the
  original Tensor allocation, interprocess `BufferHandle` imports and
  materializes a new view.
- Serialized and saturated benchmarks measure different execution models —
  compare structure, never absolute medians.
- Publisher cost grows with fanout and becomes the limiting stage in both
  Tensor transports.
- Sidecar receive is about 400 ns cheaper than `BufferHandle` receive, but
  both Tensor paths complete about the same number of samples per second.
- Device rows exclude GPU work; treat them as transport cost, not pipeline
  latency.
